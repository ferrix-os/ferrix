## 1. What there is today

* **Limits, not guarantees.** `object/quota.rs` (F-35) charges every frame of a
  program's memory and page tables to its job's slot, and F-37 charges the
  kernel heap its Linux calls hold (`ferrix_kmem`, bytes) to the same memory
  counter. A limit refuses; nothing promises. Limits may overcommit: the sum of
  `memory.max` over jobs can exceed RAM, so a QM job inside its limit can still
  take the last free frame a certified job needed.
* **The watermark** (`mm.rs` `WATERMARK`, H.QUOTA.13, L.mm.71-74, ledger 797):
  a program's frames and page tables are refused when taking them would leave
  fewer than `W` free (managed / 128, within 256 and 8192 frames); the kernel's
  own allocations are not. It protects the kernel from programs. It
  protects no job from another.
* **Reclaim** (`user/cache.rs`): a reclaim at a job's limit takes only that
  job's and its descendants' clean cache; a machine-wide one (`quota::NONE`,
  from `oom::out_of_memory` and `fs/pages.rs` `allocate_reclaiming`) takes
  anyone's. Since 7d25dff95 a machine-wide reclaim asks for the watermark's
  shortfall besides the run (`reclaim_want`). F-86 to F-88 (open) are defects
  of the job path at `memory.max`, and K12 leaves them where they are.
* **OOM kill** (`object/oom.rs`): only inside the job whose limit is full.
  When the machine itself is out, the fault fails, and nothing is killed.
* **The heap** (`mm.rs` `HEAP`, `ferrix_heap`) is one set of slab lists shared
  by every job and the kernel. Its pages come from `allocate_frames`, which
  the watermark does not hold.

## 2. The mechanism

### 2.1 A reservation

A **reservation** is a number of frames the kernel promises to one job: while
the job and its sub-jobs hold fewer frames *drawn from it* than its size, the
next frame they need is there. It sits beside the job's limit, never above
it: `reservation <= memory limit` is held at every change of either (§2.6).

The kernel keeps reservations in a fixed table in `mm.rs`,
`RESERVATIONS: [Entry; MOST_RESERVATIONS]` with `MOST_RESERVATIONS = 64`,
under the frame allocator's lock `FRAMES`. Each entry has:

| field | meaning |
|---|---|
| `size` | frames promised |
| `drawn` | frames now held that were taken against it (user frames, page tables and heap pages, §2.4) |
| `bound` | the entry belongs to a live quota slot |

and the machine keeps one more number under the same lock:

* **`O`, the outstanding promise**: `Σ max(0, size − drawn)` over the entries.
  It is the number of free frames that belong to someone. It is updated
  incrementally in the same critical section as every take and free that
  changes a `drawn`, so the identity holds at every release of `FRAMES`.

A frame taken against entry `r` is **tagged** with `r` in a side array,
`DRAWN: [AtomicU8]`, one byte per managed frame (0 = untagged; for 4 GiB it is
1 MiB, allocated at `mm::init` beside the frame records). It is written in the
`FRAMES` section that takes the frame and cleared in the one that frees it.
An atomic array in `mm.rs` is used rather than a field of `ferrix_frame`'s
`PageEntry`, so that the heap's free path can read it without `FRAMES`
(§2.4), and because `ferrix-frame` is outside the item-scoped gates (ledger
797 W2). The frame record's `owner`, the quota slot for the limit, is
unchanged. **A frame has two independent marks: whose limit it counts
against (`owner`, F-35) and whose reservation it was drawn from (`DRAWN`,
K12).**

A job's quota slot gains one field, `reservation: AtomicU8` (entry index + 1,
0 for none). A slot claimed under a parent copies the parent's, so a
partition's sub-jobs draw from the partition's reservation. That is why a
reservation is first set only on a job with no child job and no process
(§2.6). It is read with no lock wherever an allocation starts, from the
running task's group.

### 2.2 The three floors

Every frame the kernel takes passes one test, decided inside the `FRAMES` hold
that takes it, from the free count `F`, the request `n = 2^order`, `W` and `O`:

| who takes it | refused when | route |
|---|---|---|
| **a reserved job, within its reservation** (`drawn + n <= size` for its entry) | `F < n` (cannot happen while `O`'s identity holds, since `F >= O >= size − drawn >= n`) | `allocate_user_frame`, `allocate_user_run`, `ChargedTables`, and the reserved heap's page supply (§2.4); the frame is tagged, `drawn += n` and `O −= n` in the same hold |
| **a program, otherwise** (any other job, the root's programs, a reserved job past its reservation, `charging_nobody`) | `F − n < W + O` | the same program routes untagged: **the watermark raised by the promise** (L.mm.77) |
| **the kernel's own** | `F − n < O` | `allocate_frames`, `allocate_frame_run`, the shared heap's page supply, kernel stacks, roots, IOMMU, virtio, display |

So the free frames fall into three layers. The lowest `O` belong to the
reservations, the next `W` to the kernel, and the rest to anyone. The kernel's
own allocations, which today go down to zero, now stop at `O`. They are all
fallible (F-23), so a refusal there is the same `None` an empty allocator
gives. With no reservation `O = 0`, and every route behaves as it does on
`main`. That is what keeps the reference boots, and every row that
reserves nothing, unchanged.

`allocate_frames_below` (x86-64's trampoline, at boot) is untouched.

`fs/pages.rs` `reclaim_want` (7d25dff95) asks for the shortfall of `W + O`,
not of `W`, since that is the program floor now. Otherwise the floor case
of 2026-10-17 comes back at the raised floor (L.mm.82).

`mm::free_frames()` stays the raw count. `/proc/meminfo`'s `MemAvailable`
leaves out `W + O` (ledger 797's note).

### 2.3 Admission: refused at reservation time

A reservation of `n` frames for entry `r` is admitted in one `FRAMES` hold iff

    F − n >= W + O

and then `size = n` and `O += n`. Otherwise it is refused, with nothing
changed, and the caller gets `NO_MEMORY`. Before refusing, the kernel runs
one machine-wide reclaim of the shortfall `(W + O + n) − F`, **with `FRAMES`
released**, and then takes the test again once under `FRAMES` (ledger 2550
(5)). That way clean cache is not mistaken for used memory. The reclaim is
`cache::reclaim`'s bound: at most two passes round the cache list, one entry
under its lock at a time (`MEMORY-AND-TIMING.md`). Every `FRAMES` hold K12
adds is O(1): one entry, `O` and one tag.

What this guarantees is stronger than "the sum of reservations is within
physical memory". At every admission, `Σ size <= F − W`, and `F <= managed`.
So **the sum of reservations never exceeds the managed frames less the
watermark**, and a promise is only ever made out of frames that are free at
that moment. Memory a QM job already holds is never promised, because the kernel
cannot take it back. The integrator's way to reserve for certified partitions
is to reserve them first, which the partition manager does at its start
(§2.6).

Growing an admitted reservation is the same test on the difference.
Shrinking always succeeds: `size` falls, and `O` falls by what was
outstanding of the difference. If `drawn > size`, the excess frames simply
stop being covered. They stay tagged until freed, and their free credits
nothing (§2.5).

### 2.4 The kernel heap: one slab heap per reservation

A shared slab heap cannot keep a heap promise. If a reserved job's object
needs a fresh slab page and that page is drawn from its reservation, any QM
allocation of the same size class can then take the page's other slots, for
as long as it likes. Charging bytes (F-37) does not bound pages. One QM object
left in each page the reserved job grew either pins the reservation (if
pages are debited) or eats the free frames under `O` (if bytes are).
Either way the promise leaks to QM. So:

* **`HEAPS: [IrqSpinLock<Heap>; MOST_RESERVATIONS]`**, one `ferrix_heap::Heap`
  per entry, beside the shared `HEAP`. `KernelAllocator::alloc` reads the
  running group's reservation (one per-processor load and one slot load). If
  it has one and its heap can serve the request, the object comes from there.
  That heap's page supply takes frames **only as drawn frames** of its entry
  (the first floor, tagged). If the entry has no room, the request goes to the
  shared `HEAP`, as today, under the kernel's-own floor. **A reserved heap
  never takes an untagged page, and the shared heap never takes a tagged
  one.**
* **`dealloc` is routed by the tag**: the page of a slab object
  (`address & !0xFFF`), or the head frame of a large one, is looked up in
  `DRAWN`. Non-zero means `HEAPS[tag − 1]`, zero means `HEAP`. The tag cannot
  change under a live object, because it is written before the page reaches
  the heap and cleared after the heap gives the page back. So the read needs
  no lock. `HEAPS[r]`'s `deallocate_pages` frees through the tagged path
  (§2.5).
* **`mm/reserve.rs` (F-23's per-processor reserve) is unchanged.** It serves
  only when a heap refuses inside a reserved section, and its blocks are the
  shared heap's, so they are freed by tag 0 to `HEAP`.
* **Interrupt context never draws (ledger 2550 (1)).** `alloc`, and every
  frame route, tests for interrupt context first. An allocation made there
  is served from the shared heap, or untagged, under the kernel's own floor,
  whoever is current. Otherwise a QM job's network or block completion
  would take a reserved partition's pages. F-37's byte attribution stays as
  it is. Frees in interrupt context are still routed by the tag (K10 frees
  heap from its timer interrupt), so `HEAPS[r]` and the tagged free path
  are interrupt-safe (`IrqSpinLock`).
* **Who pays.** In task context the allocation draws from the reservation
  of the job the kernel charges. That is the same "whose job" function as
  K7's `charge_target` (ledger 2550 (3)): an adopted server's work for a
  reserved client draws on the client's reservation, and a reserved
  server's work for a QM client does not draw on the server's. Whichever of
  K7 and K12 lands second wires the two together, with a case. A heap object a
  reserved job's call made and another job holds (a message queued to a QM
  reader, a VMO it gave away) stays in `HEAPS[r]` until it is freed. That is
  the reserved partition's own behaviour, bounded by K7's queues and its own
  design, and stated as an AoU (§5).

F-37's byte charges are unchanged. `memory.max` still counts frames plus
heap bytes. **The reservation counts frames: user frames, tables and heap
pages, page-exact.** That is the unit K16 retypes.

### 2.5 Freeing, and the end of a reservation

* **A tagged frame freed** (`deallocate_frames`, `release_frame`, the heap's
  `deallocate_pages`): in the same `FRAMES` hold, `drawn −= n`, `O` is
  recomputed for the entry (it rises by `n` when `drawn` falls below
  `size`), and the tag is cleared. A frame freed untagged changes nothing in
  the table. The order inside the hold is free-then-credit, so `F >= O`
  holds at its end.
* **The end of a job's slot** (the last hold goes, `quota::release`): its
  entry is **unbound**. `size = 0`, so `O` loses what was outstanding of it,
  and the entry **drains**: frames and heap pages still tagged with it (a
  page-cache page charged to the gone job, an object in `HEAPS[r]` someone
  else still holds) are freed later and credit nothing. The entry and its heap
  are reused only once `drawn == 0`. A slot outlives its job while anything is
  charged to it (quota.rs), so the entry is never unbound under a live
  sub-job.
* **No free entry** (64 bound or draining): admission answers
  `LIMIT_REACHED`.

### 2.6 Who makes a reservation

* **The call:** `JOB_SET_LIMIT` (0x102B) with `resource = JOB_RESERVE` (4) and
  the size in bytes (rounded down to whole pages, as `memory.max` is).
  `JOB_GET_QUOTA` (0x102C) with `JOB_RESERVE` answers `[size, drawn,
  refused admissions]` in bytes, its three words as for the other resources.
  **No new call number.** `UNLIMITED` is refused (`INVALID_ARGS`), and 0
  shrinks to nothing but keeps the entry bound.
* **The right:** `Rights::RESERVE` (bit 8) on the handle to the job. It is
  not in `Rights::JOB`. `JOB_CREATE` gives the new job's handle `RESERVE`
  only when the parent handle it was made through has it, so the authority
  to promise memory is delegated only by whoever holds it. `SET_LIMIT` alone
  does not suffice: a limit binds the job, while a reservation binds every
  other job on the machine (§2.5's `F − W − O`).
* **Who holds it:** in the target shape (`MICROKERNEL.md` §5) **the
  partition manager**. The kernel hands it a job handle with `RESERVE`
  ("the K12 reservations to hand out"), and it makes each safety
  partition's job and reserves for it from its description, right after
  `JOB_CREATE` and before the partition's first process. **On `main` today
  no program holds `RESERVE`.** The reference configuration exercises the
  mechanism through the boot check (§3), so it is not deactivated code
  (`ITEM.md` §5).
* **Rules checked at the call**, each refused `BAD_STATE` with nothing
  changed:
  1. **First set only on an empty job**: no child job, no process, so the
     slot copy of §2.1 reaches every sub-job made later.
  2. **One level**: no ancestor and no descendant of the job holds a
     reservation. A partition's sub-jobs share its reservation, and nesting
     (carving a child's reservation out of a parent's) is K16's split (§4).
  3. **Beside the limit**: the job's own memory limit is at least the
     reservation. A `JOB_SET_LIMIT(JOB_MEMORY)` or a cgroupfs `memory.max`
     below it is refused (`BAD_STATE`, `EBUSY`).
  4. **No limit or mark above it**: no ancestor of a reserved job (below the
     root) carries a memory limit or a `memory.high` mark. Setting either on
     such an ancestor is refused. Otherwise an ancestor's limit, filled by a
     QM sibling, would refuse a charge the reservation covers, and an
     ancestor's OOM kill or `memory.high` reclaim would reach into the
     reserved job. With this rule, **only the reserved job's own limit (or a
     sub-job's) can refuse it, kill in it or reclaim from it**.
* **Who may put work into a reserved subtree (ledger 2550 (2)).** A
  sub-job's slot copies the reservation, so whatever runs in the subtree
  draws from the promise. Every way in is guarded, and a refusal changes
  nothing:
  * `PROCESS_CREATE` into a job of the subtree: refused (`ACCESS_DENIED`)
    unless the caller's own job is inside the same subtree, or the job
    handle carries `RESERVE`;
  * `JOB_CREATE` under a job of the subtree: the same rule;
  * cgroupfs (the load ring, Linux root today): a write to `cgroup.procs`
    moving a process into the subtree from outside it, and a `mkdir` under
    it, are refused `EBUSY`. A reserved subtree is not the Linux
    partition's to fill.
* **Rights (ledger 2550 (7)).** `RESERVE` is in no right set minted for a
  program. The check asserts that the job handles the kernel gives init and
  devmgr carry no `RESERVE`, and that `HANDLE_DUPLICATE` and
  `HANDLE_REPLACE` cannot add it (rights only shrink). `Rights::ALL`
  becoming `0x1FF` is an ABI change: the native-abi host tests and every
  comparison against `Rights::ALL` are updated with it.
* **Audit:** a set records `LIMIT_SET` with `resource::RESERVE` (7) and the
  size. A refused admission records `LIMIT` (refused) with `resource::RESERVE`,
  the size asked and `F − W − O` at the refusal.

### 2.7 Reclaim and the OOM path

* **Machine-wide reclaim** (`cache::reclaim(NONE, …)`, `user/cache.rs`
  `takes`) **never takes a tagged frame** unless the running group draws from
  the same entry (L.user.145). Taking a promised frame would give the
  requester nothing anyway: the frame goes back under `O`, where a program's
  floor cannot reach it. A reclaim at a job's own limit (`scope` = a slot)
  takes from that job's subtree, its own drawn frames included. That is the
  job's own business, and §2.6 rule 4 keeps every such scope inside the
  reserved job.
* **The OOM kill** stays scoped to the job whose limit is full
  (`kill_within`). By rule 4 that is the reserved job or one of its
  sub-jobs, never an ancestor shared with QM. The machine out of frames
  still kills nobody. **No new killer and no change to `oom.rs`**. The check
  shows a QM job's OOM at its own limit choosing no process of a reserved
  sibling.
* **`memory.min` / `memory.low`** keep their meaning. A reservation is not a
  `min`: it is a promise of free frames, which `min` is not.

## 3. The boot check (`present`, all four boots)

The check has two parts, because draining the machine to its floors is only
safe at stage 2, as endfield-oom's watermark check found.

**Stage 2, inside `mm/check.rs` `memory_check`** (one processor, no program),
extending the `watermark` line. Everything runs through the real routes, with
a synthetic entry bound by the check, not by a job:

1. Admission of `F − W − O + 1` frames is refused, with `O` unchanged. Then
   `F − W − O` is admitted and `O` rises by exactly that much.
2. Draining by the program route stops at `F = W + O` exactly: a frame and an
   order-1 run are refused there. The kernel's own `allocate_frames(0)`
   still succeeds down to `F = O`, and is refused at `O`.
3. Frames drawn against the entry succeed below that floor until
   `drawn = size`. Each is tagged, and `O` falls one for one to 0. The next
   draw is refused (no room, then the program floor).
4. Everything is given back. Every tag is cleared, `drawn = 0`, `O` is back to
   the size, and the unbind returns `O` to its start. The `FrameWindow` is
   exact.

**Stage 9, the `reserve` line**, after `quota`, in a check tree made by
`Job::new_root` as `quota_check` does:

5. Through `native_check`'s `Side` (the system call path): `JOB_SET_LIMIT(JOB_RESERVE)`
   on a handle without `RESERVE` is refused `ACCESS_DENIED`. With it, it is
   set. `JOB_CREATE` through a handle without `RESERVE` yields a child handle
   without it, and through one with it, a child handle with it. Rules 1 to 4
   are each refused once. `JOB_GET_QUOTA(JOB_RESERVE)` reads back size,
   drawn and refusals.
6. A reserved job and a QM sibling. The reserved job faults in pages and makes
   kernel objects. The pages are tagged with its entry, the objects' slab
   pages are in `HEAPS[r]`, and a QM allocation of the same size class does
   not land in any of those pages. Freeing routes each object back by its
   tag.
7. A clean cache page charged to the reserved job survives a machine-wide
   reclaim run from the QM sibling, and is taken by the reserved job's own
   reclaim.
8. The QM sibling at its own limit OOM-chooses among its own processes,
   never the reserved job's.
9. The tree goes. The entry drains to `drawn = 0`, `O` returns to its value
   before the check, and every quota slot is given back.

The line prints `W`, the admitted size, `O` before and after, the drawn pages
and heap pages, and the check's time. The time is measured on the four boots
and stated in `MEMORY-AND-TIMING.md`.

## 4. How K16 builds on it (ledger 2458 M3)

* **The entry is K16's untyped memory.** The `DRAWN` tag and the per-entry
  `drawn` count are exactly "memory its job has given the kernel". K16
  retypes kernel objects out of the job's drawn frames. `HEAPS[r]` is the
  first step: the reserved job's kernel objects already come only from frames
  of its own reservation. K16 replaces the slab lists with per-type
  allocators over the same drawn frames, and the shared `HEAP` with
  nothing.
* **Every job gets one.** K16 gives the Linux partition (and every other job)
  a reservation, admitted at its creation as here. The three floors then
  collapse to `W` (the kernel's boot-sized structures) and the promises. The
  "kernel's own" row of §2.2 disappears with the shared heap, so no global
  allocator is left after boot (K16's evidence).
* **Nesting is K16's split.** §2.6 rule 2 (one level) is K12's
  simplification. K16's seL4-like split carves a child's entry out of a
  parent's (`size` moved, not admitted again). The table gains a `parent`
  and a `carved` sum. The tag, the per-entry `drawn` and the identity
  `F >= O` stay as they are.
* **F-35 and F-37 map on as limits beside it.** The limit side (`owner`,
  `used`, bytes, frames plus heap bytes, hierarchical) stays the ceiling. The
  reservation side (`DRAWN`, `drawn`, frames, flat) is the floor. A frame
  carries both marks, independently. Under K16 F-37's byte charge for kernel
  objects becomes the page-exact drawn count, since an object's memory is
  then its job's own frames, and `memory.max`'s kernel part is read from
  `drawn`.
* **The tag's width.** A `u8` tag with 64 entries is K12's size. K16's
  "every job gets one" needs a wider tag (`u16` or `u32`), at 2 to 4 bytes a
  frame (ledger 2550, lesser C2).
* **Revocation** (K16's): an entry is unbound only when its slot ends, and
  drains. A forced revocation (the partition manager reclaiming a dead
  partition's memory) is K16's design, on top of the same drain.

## 5. What it does not guarantee (stated in `MEMORY-AND-TIMING.md`, the safety manual and the VA)

* **Contiguity.** The promise is a count of frames. An order > 0 draw (a run,
  a large heap object) can fail on a fragmented machine with `drawn < size`.
  K16 uses order-0 frames only.
* **The kernel's work for the job outside its calls.** Kernel threads
  (writeback, block completion, the reclaim itself) run in no job and take
  the kernel's-own floor. That is K7's (charging follows the client) and
  K16's.
* **Objects given away.** A reserved partition's objects held by others stay
  in its heap until freed (§2.4). This is an AoU on the partition's design,
  bounded by K7's queues.
* **Memory already held.** Admission cannot promise frames a QM job holds
  now. The partition manager reserves first.
* **A reserved job's heap past its entry** falls to the shared heap under
  the kernel's floor `O`, so it can take `W`, as any program's heap can
  today (ledger 797: the watermark never held the heap). K12 leaves that
  unchanged (ledger 2550, lesser C1).
* **Machine reclaim's walk** grows by at most the drawn frames it skips.
* **Entries held by stale frames.** A drained entry is bound again only once
  nothing is drawn from it, so frames kept alive after their job (a K5
  waiter on a VMO, a page-cache page charged to the gone job) keep it out of
  the 64 until they go (ledger 2783, lesser C1).
* **F-86 to F-88** (the job path at `memory.max`) are not addressed. A reserved
  job reaching its own limit behaves as any job does there.
